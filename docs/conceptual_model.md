# Conceptual model: bridging adults

> Draft for discussion. Citations marked (checked) were confirmed against publisher or
> library records on 2026-11-14; the rest still need checking before external use.

## The idea in one sentence

Young people from low-income families gain from relationships that **bridge class lines**,
especially with supportive non-parental adults. These relationships open access to
information, role models and belief in their own ability (**self-efficacy**), and a sense
that they **belong**. Those in turn support school engagement, graduation and, later,
economic mobility.

## Model

```
 STRUCTURE                CROSS-CLASS CONNECTION            MECHANISMS               OUTCOMES
 ----------               ----------------------            ----------               --------
 Family income   ──┐      (a) EXPOSURE                      Information &            School engagement
 Parent education  │          meeting people of other       navigation               Grades / graduation
 Adversity (ACEs)  ├────►     classes (schools, activities) ─► Role models &      ──► Hope / flourishing
 School & place    │      (b) BRIDGING                         aspirations            Adult income
 SES mix         ──┘          ties actually forming:        Self-efficacy            (Project 01)
                              friendships across class,     Belonging
                              mentors (non-parental adults)

 MODERATORS: compensation (ties matter most where family resources are fewest);
             relationship quality and length; fit between mentor and youth.
```

Two distinctions drive the predictions:

- **Exposure vs. bridging.** Being in mixed settings is not the same as forming ties there.
  Chetty et al. (2022) separate *exposure* (how many higher-income people are around) from
  *friending bias* (whether ties form given exposure). Mentoring is a deliberate bridging
  intervention, so the model predicts that bridging matters beyond exposure.
- **Compensation vs. accumulation.** If non-parental adults supply resources that low-income
  families have less access to, their association with outcomes should be **larger** for
  low-income or high-adversity youth (compensation). If advantages pile up, it should be
  larger for advantaged youth (accumulation).

## Revised model after testing

```
 Supportive / bridging adults ──► Self-efficacy ──────────► School outcomes (achievement, engagement, graduation)
          │      (mastery, encouragement)  ▲  (strongest link)
          │                                │ small
          └──────────────────────────► Belonging ──► Wellbeing (less hopelessness), grades
                                           (its own outcome; weak direct link to achievement)
 Hardship dampens the link to hope/hopelessness. Anxiety is the largest drag on self-efficacy.
```

The original drawing put belonging *before* self-efficacy on one chain. PISA shows two
mostly separate channels: supportive adults relate to self-efficacy directly (and
self-efficacy to achievement), and to belonging, which matters for wellbeing. That changes
what a program should emphasize: confidence-building (mastery experiences, encouragement,
visible role models) for school outcomes; connection for wellbeing; and practical supports
where hardship is high.

## What public data can't do

- **No public dataset measures cross-class mentors directly.** The NSCH item asks whether a
  child has "at least one other adult ... who they can rely on for advice or guidance", not
  that adult's background. So NSCH tests the *supportive-adult* arm and the Social Capital
  Atlas tests the *cross-class* arm. Linking them, by asking about the class background of a
  youth's mentors in aggregate categories, is something Project 03's surveys could collect.
- All three datasets capture each young person (or school) once, so the results are
  associations. Children with supportive adults may differ in ways not measured.
- NSCH answers come from parents, not youth.
