# 02 - Mathematics of Mentorship: bridging adults

**Question:** How do relationships that cross class lines, especially with supportive
non-parental adults, relate to self-efficacy, belonging and youth outcomes? The project
builds a research-based conceptual model and tests its predictions with public data.

**Status:** complete (2026-11-13). Conceptual model; P1-P6 tested in four national datasets
(NSCH, Social Capital Atlas + EDFacts + NCES, YRBS, PISA); revised model; national benchmarks
and measurement recommendations handed to Project 03. Before going public: verify the
citations and confirm OECD/Urban Institute terms.

> **Exploratory.** Check every figure and citation against its original source before
> using it in funder reports, grant applications or public material. Everything here is an
> association measured at one point in time, not a causal effect.

## The model in brief

See [docs/conceptual_model.md](docs/conceptual_model.md). Low-income youth gain from ties
that **bridge class lines**. Two distinctions drive the predictions: **exposure** (being
around people of other classes) vs. **bridging** (ties actually forming, which mentoring
does on purpose), and **compensation** (ties matter more where family resources are fewer)
vs. **accumulation** (advantages pile up).

## Key findings

Effect sizes are differences in percentage points, with 95% confidence intervals.

**P1 Access gap: supported.** 80% of children below the poverty line have a non-parental
adult they can rely on, against 94% of children at 400%+ of the poverty line (NSCH 2024,
ages 6-17). Lower-income children also take part in far fewer sports, clubs and lessons
(56% vs. 92%). Accounting for those activities shrinks the income gap in having a mentor by
about a third (from -10.0 to -6.5 points below the poverty line). That fits the idea that
structured activities are where children meet these adults.

**P2 Association: supported.** Having such an adult goes with flourishing **+14 points**
(CI 10.5 to 18), engagement in school **+12** (9 to 16) and staying hopeful **+6.5** (4 to 9),
after adjusting for income, parent education, family structure, adverse experiences and
demographics.

**P3 Compensation: mixed, depending on the outcome.**
- *School engagement: leans toward compensation.* The mentor difference is larger for
  children with 2+ adverse experiences (+11 points more, CI 1 to 21) and trends larger at
  lower incomes (15 points below the poverty line vs. 9 at 400%+).
- *Hope: accumulation.* The mentor difference is smaller for low-income children (-7 points,
  CI -11 to -2). It is about 2-4 points below 200% of the poverty line and about 10 above.
- *Flourishing:* no clear difference by income or adversity.

**P4 Bridging beyond exposure: supported.** Across 8,934 public high schools, schools where
lower-income students befriend higher-income peers more (lower friending bias) graduate
more of their economically disadvantaged students: **+0.5 points per SD** less friending
bias (CI 0.3 to 0.75). That is net of exposure (+1.7 per SD), school poverty, size and state.
The friending effect is **about four times larger in socioeconomically mixed schools** (-1.2
vs. -0.26 per SD), as the model predicts: bridging matters where there is someone to bridge
to. The effects are modest relative to an average rate of about 86%.

**P5 Belonging: supported, with the same split as P3.** Teens who feel close to people at
school are **+12 points** more likely to report mostly A's and B's and **-16 points** less
likely to report persistent sadness or hopelessness (YRBS 2023). Teens facing household
hardship (14%) feel less belonging (48% vs. 56%). The grades link is the same with or
without hardship, but the link with less hopelessness is much weaker under hardship (-7 vs.
-18 points).

**P6 Self-efficacy pathway: supported, but it runs differently than drawn.** PISA 2022
(3,943 U.S. 15-year-olds; standardized coefficients adjusted for family SES and gender; BRR
replicate weights and all 10 plausible values):
- Supportive teacher relationships go with **belonging (+0.33 SD, CI 0.28 to 0.37)** and with
  **self-efficacy directly (+0.20, CI 0.16 to 0.23)**. Self-efficacy is the strongest
  correlate of math achievement in the model (**+0.39 SD**).
- **The route to achievement runs through self-efficacy, not belonging.** About 77% of the
  link between teacher relationships and math (total +0.10 SD) runs through self-efficacy
  directly (indirect +0.075). The route through belonging is tiny (+0.009), and belonging
  has no positive direct link to math once self-efficacy is known (-0.06).
- Of the things that go with self-efficacy, math anxiety is the biggest (-0.32 SD), then
  family SES (+0.18), teacher relationships (+0.13) and family support (+0.07). Belonging
  adds almost nothing (+0.03, CI -0.01 to 0.07).
- **Cross-class exposure doesn't cost disadvantaged students confidence.** Disadvantaged
  students (bottom national ESCS quartile, n = 985) in higher-SES schools score higher in
  math (+0.14 SD per SD of school SES mix, CI 0.05 to 0.24). They show **no "big fish,
  little pond" drop** in self-efficacy (+0.00 at the same math score) or belonging (+0.03).
- Teacher relationships go with belonging and self-efficacy **equally strongly for
  disadvantaged and other students** (difference -0.01 and +0.04, both CIs include 0).
- **The pattern holds internationally.** In all 65 countries/economies with these measures,
  teacher relationships → belonging, belonging → self-efficacy, teacher relationships →
  self-efficacy, and self-efficacy → math are positive. The direct belonging → math link
  goes either way (positive in 37%).

### What it adds up to

1. **Supportive and cross-class ties consistently go with better school-related outcomes,**
   and those benefits are at least as large for disadvantaged youth. That supports
   mentoring aimed at engagement, persistence and graduation.
2. **Supportive adults likely work through two separate channels:**
   - **Belonging,** a wellbeing outcome in its own right.
   - **Self-efficacy,** the channel linked to achievement.

   The model is revised accordingly (see [docs/conceptual_model.md](docs/conceptual_model.md)).
   Mentoring that builds *mastery and confidence* (practice, encouragement, seeing someone
   like you succeed: Bandura's sources of self-efficacy) is more likely to show up in school
   outcomes than mentoring that only builds connection.
3. **Emotional outcomes (hope, hopelessness) benefit less under poverty or hardship.**
   Relationships may not offset the stress of material hardship by themselves. A program may
   need to pair mentoring with practical supports, and should not promise wellbeing gains
   from mentoring alone. Anxiety is the largest drag on self-efficacy, which makes it a
   practical target.
4. **Access is unequal at every step:** fewer mentors, fewer structured activities and less
   belonging for low-income youth. **Building ties within mixed settings** (lower friending
   bias) is where the school-level evidence points, and mixed settings don't appear to cost
   disadvantaged students their confidence.

### Recommendations for Project 03's measures

| Recommendation | Why (finding) | Status in Project 03 |
|---|---|---|
| Make self-efficacy a primary outcome, measured domain-specifically | Strongest correlate of achievement; carries most of the teacher-relationship link (P6) | Done: SE1-SE6 (Bandura-style, domain-specific) |
| Keep belonging primary, but as a wellbeing outcome, not a proxy for achievement | Linked to grades and less hopelessness (P5), weak direct link to achievement (P6) | Done: BE1-BE4; BE1 benchmarked to YRBS |
| Measure access itself (supportive adults, bridging ties) | Large income gaps in mentors and activities (P1); bridging matters beyond exposure (P4) | Done: SA1-SA5; SA1 benchmarked to NSCH |
| Keep hope secondary; no wellbeing promises from mentoring alone | Hope/hopelessness respond least under hardship (P3, P5) | Done; sensitive items need a safeguarding protocol |
| Add a short stress/anxiety item at pilot | Anxiety is the largest negative correlate of self-efficacy (P6) | Done: pilot item AX1 (not scored in any scale until piloted) |
| Plan for effects of 0.1-0.15 SD and analyze equity by hardship | Associations are similar across groups; program effects are diluted by early closures | Done: power analysis, hardship subgroups |

## Method notes

- **NSCH:** weighted linear probability models with robust SEs (ignores strata, so slightly
  conservative). Each of the 6 income imputations is fitted and combined with Rubin's rules.
  "Flourishing" uses the Data Resource Center's definition for ages 6-17 (curiosity, finishes
  tasks, stays calm: each "always" or "usually"). "School engagement" = cares about doing well
  and does all homework (each always/usually). "Hopeful" = all or most of the time.
- **High schools:** public schools with 12-digit NCES IDs present in all three sources, at
  least 100 students and at least 20 students in the disadvantaged cohort. Weighted by
  disadvantaged cohort size; state fixed effects; SEs clustered by district. Traits are
  standardized (weighted).
- **YRBS:** CDC's derived yes/no variables (QN); weighted; SEs clustered by PSU.
- **PISA:** U.S. students with all indices present. Indices standardized (student-weighted);
  math in SD units. Path model as three weighted regressions (belonging; self-efficacy; math),
  each adjusted for ESCS and gender, with indirect effects as products of paths. Uncertainty
  combines BRR sampling variance (Fay 0.5: sum of squared replicate deviations / 20) with
  plausible-value variance (Rubin). School SES mix = leave-one-out weighted mean ESCS of
  sampled schoolmates (schools with 5+ sampled students). International estimates use final
  weights only (point estimates), for the 65 systems with these indices and at least 1,000
  students.

## Caveats

- **Cross-sectional throughout:** children with mentors, or schools with more cross-class
  friendship, may differ in ways not measured. Some of the association may run the other
  way (thriving children may attract mentors).
- **The NSCH mentor item doesn't say who the adult is,** so it can't tell whether the tie
  crosses class lines. Project 03's surveys could ask about this in aggregate categories.
- **The friendship measures describe students a few years ahead of the 2019 graduates** and
  only those on Facebook.
- **Graduation rates are reported in ranges,** so small schools are measured less precisely
  (weighting by cohort size reduces this).
- **Hardship in YRBS** is a single item about basic needs; it is not family income.
- **PISA's paths are associations from one survey,** so the ordering (teachers → belonging →
  self-efficacy → math) is the model's assumption, not something the data prove. Confident
  students may also see teachers more warmly. PISA measures *teachers*, the closest
  supportive non-parental adults it covers; mentors may work differently.
